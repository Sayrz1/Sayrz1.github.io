NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, team project

Lunar Navigator

A web app for choosing where and when to land on the Moon. Pick a site, such as Apollo 11 or an Artemis III candidate near the south pole, and a date. It shows when the Sun sets behind the terrain and when Earth drops out of radio contact.

  • Real horizons from NASA LOLA terrain, traced along 720 directions around each site in a Web Worker so the page never freezes
  • Sun and Earth positions from astronomy-engine, checked against the NASA JPL Horizons REST API to within 0.003°
  • A Python pipeline (NumPy, Pillow) that turns raw NASA PDS terrain files into tiles the browser can stream
  • A year-ahead landing calendar, battery estimate, Canvas charts, an "Ask Navi" question box and a full Arabic version

Final year project, NLP

LexiLift

A Chrome extension that rewrites any web page into simpler English at a CEFR level you choose (A2, B1 or B2). Nothing is sent to the internet: a local FastAPI backend serves the models.

  • Fine-tuned six models with Hugging Face Transformers and PyTorch: T5-small and BART-base, each on raw, BERTScore-filtered and NLI-filtered data, tuned with Optuna on CUDA GPUs
  • Built the training set by combining ASSET, Newsela and TurkCorpus, with pandas profiling reports for every filter step
  • The deployed T5 model scores 56.43 SARI and changes numbers in only 0.60% of outputs, backed up by a human evaluation
  • A meaning gate compares sentence embeddings (sentence-transformers) and rejects rewrites that drift too far
  • A FastAPI and Pydantic backend with a result cache, a Chrome MV3 extension, and an ONNX export that runs 1.5 to 2 times faster on CPU

Here's the method on three ads, written the way a lot of small shops write them. The shops are made up, borrowed from my motion films. Pick an ad, then flip between before and after.

Instagram post Halfmoon Coffee, Hackney

Hhalfmoon.coffeeSponsored

halfmoon.coffee Come and try our AMAZING coffee!!! Best coffee in town, made with love. Visit us today!! #coffee #love #instagood #coffeetime

halfmoon.coffee When was your flat white roasted? Ours was roasted this morning, on Mare Street in Hackney. You'll smell it from the bus stop. A flat white is £3.20, ready by the time you've tapped your card. Show this post before 9 on a weekday and your pastry's on us. Halfmoon Coffee, open from 7am. #halfmooncoffee #hackneycoffee #flatwhite

AIDAFor people walking down Mare Street at 8 who already buy coffee somewhere else.

  1. Open with a question only this shop can answer. "Best coffee in town" fits any café. Roasting on Mare Street each morning belongs to Halfmoon, and the first line carries "flat white" and "Hackney", the words people search.
  2. Show the price. A flat white is an everyday buy. If the price is missing, that's one more reason to scroll past.
  3. Give one reason to come tomorrow. A free pastry before 9 turns "I'll try it some day" into a plan for the walk to work. Three hashtags, the shop's own plus two people in Hackney search, replace four that millions of other posts use.

"Flat white", "Mare Street" and "Hackney" all sit in the first 91 characters, before Instagram cuts the caption at 125. Brand name at the call to action. Post it with a Mare Street location tag and alt text, which Instagram search also reads.

Google search ad Miller Family Plumbing, Columbus

MMiller Family PlumbingSponsored

millerplumbing.com

Professional Plumbing Services | Quality Work | Affordable Prices

We offer a wide range of plumbing services for residential and commercial customers. Contact us today for all your plumbing needs!

millerplumbing.com › emergency › columbus

Emergency Plumber, Columbus OH | At Your Door in 60 Minutes | $89 Flat Call-Out Fee

Family-run since 1998. You get the price before we pick up a wrench. Answering calls until 10pm, 7 days a week. Call for a plumber today.

PASFor someone in Columbus with water on the floor, searching on their phone right now.

  1. Use the words people type. Someone with water on the floor searches "emergency plumber" and their town. Google rates ads on how closely they match the search, so those words open the first headline.
  2. Promise something a customer can hold you to. Most plumbing ads say "quality work". A 60-minute arrival and a flat $89 fee are promises.
  3. Answer the fear before the call. Their real worry is a surprise bill, so the description says they get the price before any work starts.

Headlines of 30, 26 and 21 characters (limit 30). Two descriptions of 68 characters each (limit 90). Display path /emergency/columbus repeats the search words (limit 15 each).

Website, About page Crumb & Co., Bristol

About Us | Crumb & Co.

About Us

Crumb & Co. is a family-run bakery offering a wide range of high-quality breads, cakes and pastries. We pride ourselves on using only the finest ingredients. Customer satisfaction is our top priority.

Sourdough bakery in Bristol | Crumb & Co.

Sourdough bakery on Gloucester Road, Bristol

Our sourdough proves for 36 hours, and most mornings the last loaf has gone by eleven. Order by 8pm and we'll keep one for you, still warm, at 7.

Save me a loaf

BABFor locals who searched "sourdough bakery Bristol", walked over, and found the shelves empty.

  1. Put the search words in the heading. People look for "sourdough bakery Bristol", so the page title and main heading use those words. Nobody searches for "customer satisfaction".
  2. Explain "high quality" with how it's made. Any bakery can claim the finest ingredients. Thirty-six hours of proving is something a reader can picture.
  3. Turn the empty shelf into the offer. Selling out by eleven proves the bread is good, and it's also the reader's worry. "Save me a loaf" settles it in one tap.

Page title of 41 characters, short enough to show in full on Google. The main heading names the product and the area. Meta description of 121 characters, under Google's roughly 155: "Sourdough proved for 36 hours, baked on Gloucester Road, Bristol. Order by 8pm and we'll keep a warm loaf for you at 7am."

How I rewrite an ad

  1. Open on a scene they knowThe first line has one job: stop the scroll. I start with a moment from the reader's day, in the words they'd search.
  2. Write it like a chatAfter each line I hear what a friend would ask back, like "how much?" or "is it far?". The next line answers it.
  3. Answer the fear earlyA surprise bill or an empty shelf. I say the worry in their words, then settle it with a fact.
  4. Ask for one easy thingOne action and a reason to take it today. If people have to work out what to do next, they don't do it.
